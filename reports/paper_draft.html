Do violence detectors generalise? A leakage-controlled, cross-dataset evaluation of video foundation models and lightweight networks on RWF-2000, SFD and AIRTLab

Arpit Thakur, Vishnu
25 November 2026. Code, splits and results: github.com/ArpitThakur7/suraksha-net-violence-detection

Abstract

Published violence-detection results on RWF-2000 range from 77% to almost 100% accuracy under protocols that are often unstated, and few models are tested on data from a different source. We audit three public datasets (RWF-2000, the Surveillance Camera Fight dataset and AIRTLab; 2,650 clips) at the level of file hashes, perceptual near-duplicates and source videos, and find 57 RWF-2000 clips that share YouTube source videos with SFD. On grouped, hash-locked splits we compare frozen video foundation models with linear classifiers, a partially fine-tuned VideoMAE, and lightweight fine-tuned networks, choosing every threshold on validation data and reporting group-bootstrap confidence intervals. The partially fine-tuned VideoMAE reaches a test ROC-AUC of 0.972 (91.2% accuracy) on the pooled datasets and keeps 0.963 on RWF-2000 when trained only on the other two. Lightweight models lose more under this leave-one-dataset-out shift (mean ROC-AUC 0.782 for MotionLite against 0.887 for the fine-tuned VideoMAE), but run 8× faster on a CPU with 29× fewer parameters. Distilling VideoMAE into MotionLite raises its ROC-AUC on unseen AIRTLab from 0.762 to 0.829, above the fine-tuned VideoMAE (0.792). Contrary to our expectation, random versus grouped splits changed scores by at most a few hundredths of ROC-AUC on these data; domain shift, not split leakage, is the dominant effect. A live monitoring prototype runs the distilled model on a camera stream. We release the audit manifests, locked splits and code.

1. Introduction

Automatic violence detection in surveillance video is usually benchmarked on RWF-2000 [1], with smaller sets such as SFD [2] and AIRTLab [3] used for additional results. Reported RWF-2000 accuracies span a wide range, and lightweight models under stated protocols cluster around 82–90% [4, 5, 6], while larger models report 94–96% [7, 8]. Several practices make these numbers hard to compare: random clip-level splits of datasets whose clips come from longer source videos [6], frame-level folds [9], and selecting models or thresholds on the test split. Cross-dataset tests exist [6, 10, 11] but none we found covers SFD and AIRTLab with grouped splits.

This paper asks a practical question: which kind of model still works on footage from a dataset it never saw, and at what computational cost? Our contributions are:

  1. a source-level audit of RWF-2000, SFD and AIRTLab (hashes, perceptual near-duplicates, source videos and AIRTLab camera pairs) that finds cross-dataset overlap between RWF-2000 and SFD not reported before, to our knowledge, together with grouped, hash-locked splits for ten protocols;
  2. a leave-one-dataset-out (LODO) evaluation with validation-only model and threshold selection and group-bootstrap confidence intervals;
  3. a comparison of frozen foundation models [12, 13], partial fine-tuning, and lightweight networks, including distillation from VideoMAE into a 3M-parameter model [14], with measured CPU and GPU latency;
  4. a bias study of random versus grouped splits, which on these data shows only a small effect.

2. Data and audit

Datasets. RWF-2000 [1] has 2,000 five-second clips of real fights and non-fights collected from YouTube surveillance footage, with an official 1,600/400 split. SFD [2] has 300 two-second surveillance clips (150 fight, 150 non-fight), also collected from YouTube. AIRTLab [3] has 350 staged clips (230 violent, 120 non-violent) recorded by two cameras, with non-violent actions chosen to resemble violence (hugs, claps).

Audit. Every file is hashed with SHA-256 (2650 clips; 5 exact duplicates removed), decoded, and summarised with perceptual hashes. Clips are merged into global groups when they are near-duplicates, come from the same source video (RWF-2000 source IDs and SFD's list of YouTube links), or are the two camera views of one AIRTLab scene. This gives 1,122 groups, 14 of which span more than one dataset. Ten YouTube source videos appear in both RWF-2000 and SFD, covering 57 RWF-2000 clips (including clips in RWF-2000's official validation split) and SFD clips in its training and test splits; 57 of the 447 cross-dataset clip pairs from these sources are visual near-duplicates.

Splits. RWF-2000 keeps its official split as the test set; SFD and AIRTLab are split 70/15/15 stratified by label and group. Inside a protocol, a train or validation clip whose group also appears in a higher-precedence split (test, then validation) is dropped, so no group spans two splits. Every split file is hashed into a lock file that training checks before reading it. Table 1 lists the protocols.

3. Methods

Input. Clips are decoded once at 15 fps into a JPEG frame cache (short side 256 px). A training sample is 16 frames drawn from a random 60–100% segment, randomly resized and cropped to 224 px, flipped, colour-jittered and occasionally converted to grey; evaluation uses 16 evenly spaced frames and a centre crop.

Frozen foundation models + linear probes. VideoMAE-B fine-tuned on Kinetics-400 [12], CLIP ViT-B/16 [13] (per-frame image embeddings averaged over 8 frames) and R(2+1)D-18 [15] encode each clip over four evenly spaced temporal windows with all weights frozen. A logistic-regression classifier on standardised features is trained per protocol, each window being one training sample; validation and test probabilities are averaged over windows. Pooled and LODO protocols weight samples so that every (dataset, label) cell carries equal weight. The regularisation strength is chosen from {0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1} by validation ROC-AUC. We also concatenate VideoMAE and CLIP features. The probes are deterministic, so they are run once. Recent versions of the transformers library no longer load VideoMAE's attention biases (stored as q_bias/v_bias); we copy them into the query and value biases with a zero key bias, as in the original model.

Partial fine-tuning. For VideoMAE-B we freeze the patch embedding and the first 8 of 12 transformer blocks and train the last 4 blocks, the final norm and a new two-way head (28.4M trainable parameters) with learning rate 5×10-5.

Lightweight networks. MobileNetV3-TSM inserts temporal shift modules into an ImageNet MobileNetV3-Large. MotionLite (3.0M parameters) adds a frame-difference stream to the shifted RGB stream, fuses the two with a learned gate in the middle of the network and pools over time with attention. Its components are published individually [4, 5, 16]; we treat it as a strong lightweight baseline, not as a new architecture.

Distillation. MotionLite is also trained with the loss (1 − α)·CE(y) + α·CE(pt), α = 0.5, where pt is the VideoMAE probe's probability for the clip [14]. To avoid memorised targets, pt is out-of-fold: the protocol's training clips are split into five group-stratified folds and each clip's target comes from a probe trained on the other four. Only the protocol's own training and validation clips are used, so a LODO teacher never sees the held-out dataset.

Training and selection. AdamW (weight decay 0.05), learning rate 3×10-4 unless stated, cosine schedule with 2 warm-up epochs, label smoothing 0.1, effective batch 32, mixed precision, at most 40 epochs with early stopping after 6 epochs without a better validation ROC-AUC. The checkpoint with the best validation ROC-AUC is kept, and the decision threshold is the value on a 0.05–0.95 grid that maximises validation F1. The test split of each protocol is scored once, with the frozen threshold.

Statistics. 95% confidence intervals use a percentile bootstrap that resamples whole groups (1,000 resamples) [17]. Where several training seeds have finished we report the mean and standard deviation across seeds.

4.2 Generalisation to an unseen dataset

Under LODO (Table 3) every model loses accuracy relative to the pooled protocol, but large pretrained models lose far less. Trained on SFD and AIRTLab only (531 clips), the fine-tuned VideoMAE still reaches 0.963 on RWF-2000. The concatenated VideoMAE + CLIP probe has the best mean over the three held-out datasets (0.911). The lightweight models fall to a mean of 0.789 (MobileNetV3-TSM) and 0.782 (MotionLite). The SFD and AIRTLab test sets have only 47 and 52 clips, so their intervals are wide (Figure 2). Accuracy at the frozen threshold drops more than ROC-AUC: for instance MotionLite on unseen AIRTLab ranks clips with ROC-AUC 0.762 but reaches only 62.5% accuracy, because the threshold chosen on the training domains does not transfer.

4.3 Does split leakage inflate scores?

We expected random splits of RWF-2000 to score higher than grouped splits, and the pooled protocol without the group rule to score higher than with it. Table 4 shows that the differences are small and inconsistent in sign: within about 0.01–0.03 ROC-AUC and inside the confidence intervals. The two RWF-2000 splits also have different test sets (240 and 207 clips), which limits the comparison. On these data the large effect is the shift between datasets (Section 4.2), not leakage inside a dataset. We report this negative result because the opposite claim is common.

4.4 Accuracy versus cost, and distillation

Table 5 lists model size and latency for one 16-frame clip on the laptop's RTX 4050 GPU (fp16) and on its CPU (fp32, 4 threads). On the CPU, the setting of inexpensive CCTV recorders without a GPU, MotionLite needs 156 ms per clip against 1286 ms for VideoMAE (8× faster) with 29× fewer parameters, at a cost of about 0.08 pooled ROC-AUC (Figure 3). Distillation from VideoMAE changed MotionLite's ROC-AUC by +0.003 (pooled), +0.013 (RWF-2000), +0.001 (unseen RWF-2000), +0.004 (unseen SFD), +0.067 (unseen AIRTLab), each a mean over two training runs. The gains on unseen AIRTLab and on RWF-2000 are larger than the spread between runs; on the other protocols the change is within that spread. On unseen AIRTLab the distilled model (0.829) also scores above the fine-tuned VideoMAE (0.792) with 29× fewer parameters.

4.5 Live monitoring prototype

To check that the lightweight model works on a stream, the distilled MotionLite trained on the pooled protocol runs in a local web application that reads a webcam, an RTSP camera or a recording played back in real time. Frames go through the same test-time preprocessing as in evaluation (15 fps, short side 256, centre crop 224). Every second the model scores the last 4 seconds (16 frames), and an alert with a snapshot is raised when 3 of the last 5 scores reach the threshold chosen on validation data, with at most one alert per camera every 20 seconds. On a 30-second sequence assembled from held-out RWF-2000 test clips (alternating non-violent and violent scenes), non-violent scenes scored about 0.05–0.08 and violent scenes 0.92–0.96, and the alert fired about 5 seconds after the violent scene began; one forward pass took about 13 ms on the laptop GPU. This is a functional check, not an evaluation: false alarms per hour on long, continuous footage remain to be measured.

5. Discussion and limitations

Why large pretrained models generalise better. VideoMAE and CLIP were trained on far more and more varied video and images than our 1,728 pooled training clips; their features change less across camera, resolution and staging differences. Fine-tuning only the last blocks adapts VideoMAE to the task without discarding that prior.

Pretraining overlap. Kinetics-400 and the violence datasets are both drawn largely from YouTube. We cannot rule out that some source videos appear in both, which would favour the Kinetics-pretrained models; a similar effect is documented for zero-shot action recognition [18].

Small test sets and runs. The SFD and AIRTLab test sets have only 47 and 52 clips. The fine-tuned VideoMAE has three training runs and both MotionLite variants have two; MobileNetV3-TSM and the deterministic frozen probes have one.

Thresholds under shift. A threshold tuned on the training domains transfers poorly to a new dataset even when ranking quality (ROC-AUC) holds; deployments should recalibrate on local footage.

6. Conclusion

With leakage-controlled splits, a partially fine-tuned VideoMAE detects violence with ROC-AUC 0.972 on three pooled datasets and 0.963 on RWF-2000 without training on it, while small fine-tuned networks lose much more on unseen datasets. Lightweight models remain an order of magnitude cheaper on a CPU, which keeps them relevant for edge deployment. On these datasets the measurable problem is domain shift rather than split leakage, and evaluation should include a dataset the model has never seen. Next steps are deploying the distilled model on edge hardware next to a camera and measuring false alarms on long, continuous footage.
